TITLE:
Knowledge Structure, Exit Mode, and Democratic Sanctions: How Firm-Level
Intellectual Capital Shapes Multinational Withdrawal from Hostile Markets

1.  WHAT IS THE RESEARCH QUESTION?

When geopolitical crises force multinational corporations (MNCs) to exit a
host country, firms face a consequential choice: sell their local operations
to domestic buyers, or abandon them outright by walking away. This choice is
not merely a financial one. Selling transfers organizational knowledge,
proprietary technology, and operational capabilities to buyers in the
adversarial regime, while walking away destroys value but prevents knowledge
leakage. This paper asks:

   How does a multinational's knowledge structure determine its mode of exit
   from a hostile market under geopolitical duress, and do democratic
   sanctions amplify this sorting?

We study this question in the context of the mass corporate withdrawal from
Russia following the February 2022 invasion of Ukraine -- the largest
geopolitically motivated exit event since the anti-apartheid divestment
movement of the 1980s. We decompose the question into three sub-inquiries:

(a) Do IP-heavy firms (those with high patent intensity relative to assets)
systematically choose to sell their Russian operations, while operationally
embedded firms (those with labor-intensive, tacit-knowledge-dependent
operations) walk away?

(b) Do EU sanctions sharpen this knowledge-exit-mode sorting, and if so,
through what mechanism?

(c) Do home-country institutions -- reflecting the political economy of the
firm's domicile -- moderate exit behavior conditional on knowledge type?

All three sub-inquiries are comparative statics of a single formal model:
exit as a fire-sale problem with inalienable human capital (a divestment
version of Shleifer and Vishny, 1992, combined with Hart and Moore, 1994).
A parent forced to exit sells iff the transferable fraction of subsidiary
value clears one cutoff:

   sell  iff  theta(k, h) > theta* = (tau - w) / V

where theta(k, h) is the fraction of going-concern value V that survives
the transfer -- increasing in codified-knowledge intensity k (patents,
documented processes, which are alienable and transfer with the legal
entity) and decreasing in tacit/human-capital intensity h (knowledge
embodied in employees, who can quit and whose firm-specific
complementarities are destroyed by the transfer); tau is the transaction
cost of a sale and w the wind-down cost of walking away. Firms differ in
the private value of retaining the subsidiary, V_i, distributed according
to a continuous log-concave distribution F, so the deterministic cutoff
becomes a probabilistic sell/no-sell decision and the binary-choice models
we estimate are the model's likelihood. Question (a) is the slope of theta
in (k, h); question (b) is sanctions raising tau and hence theta*;
question (c) is two opposite-signed shifts of theta* -- coalition
alignment raises tau (less selling), stakeholder-pressure institutions
raise w (more selling) -- a testable contrast. The theoretical grounding
connects the knowledge-based view of the firm (codified versus tacit
knowledge) to the economics of fire sales.


2.  WHAT IS THE RESEARCH GAP AND CONTRIBUTION TO THE ACADEMIC LITERATURE?

This study addresses three gaps at the intersection of international
business, political economy, and the knowledge-based view of the firm.

Gap 1: Geopolitical triggers in the market exit literature. The
international business literature on foreign market exit has focused
predominantly on economic determinants -- declining profitability, rising
liability of foreignness, sunk cost considerations, and host-country
institutional deterioration (Benito and Welch, 1997; Berry, 2013; Soule,
Swaminathan, and Tihanyi, 2014). While some work examines political risk as
a motive for exit (Dai, Eden, and Beamish, 2013; Witte et al., 2020), this
literature has largely treated exit as a binary decision (stay vs. leave)
rather than examining the mode of exit -- a distinction that carries
profoundly different implications for knowledge transfer and sanctions
effectiveness. The unprecedented scale of the Russia withdrawal (over 1,000
firms) provides a unique natural experiment to study exit mode variation.

Gap 2: Micro-mechanisms of sanctions effectiveness. The political economy
literature on economic sanctions overwhelmingly studies aggregate outcomes:
trade flows, GDP effects, regime behavior change, and welfare costs (Hufbauer
et al., 2007; Felbermayr et al., 2020; Bapat et al., 2013). A growing
literature examines firm-level effects of sanctions on trade and investment
(Crozet and Hinz, 2020; Ahn and Ludema, 2020), but no existing work
examines how sanctions interact with firm-level knowledge characteristics to
shape exit behavior. This gap matters because sanctions are a primary tool
of democratic resilience -- the mechanism through which democratic coalitions
respond to autocratic aggression -- yet their firm-level behavioral channels
remain poorly understood.

Gap 3: The knowledge-based view meets geopolitics. The KBV literature
(Grant, 1996; Kogut and Zander, 1993; Nonaka, 1994) has extensively studied
how knowledge characteristics shape firm boundaries, entry mode, and
subsidiary governance, but has not been applied to the exit context. This is
a significant omission: if knowledge characteristics determine entry mode
(Hennart, 1988; Brouthers, 2002), they should logically also shape exit
mode, particularly when exit is involuntary and time-pressured.

Our contribution bridges these three literatures. We provide a minimal
formal model -- exit as a fire-sale problem with inalienable human capital
-- in which one cutoff, theta(k, h) > theta*, organizes all three
questions, and we measure the two knowledge dimensions separately: parent
patent-stock percentile (codified, k) and subsidiary labor-intensity
percentile (tacit, h). The separation is itself an empirical finding: an
earlier symmetric composite of the two components failed a decomposition
test (the patent component does all the work in the exit-mode margin),
which is exactly what the model's asymmetric theta predicts -- the sale
margin prices what transfers, and what transfers is the codified
component. We show that codified-knowledge intensity predicts selling over
abandonment, examine whether sanctions steepen this sorting through the
transaction-cost channel, and test the opposite-signed institutional
shifts. In doing so, we provide the first micro-level evidence on how firm
knowledge characteristics mediate the effectiveness of sanctions as a tool
of democratic resilience, contributing simultaneously to the IB exit
literature, the political economy of sanctions, and the applied KBV.


3.  WHY IS IT IMPORTANT FOR BUSINESSES AND POLICY MAKERS TO CLOSE THIS
    RESEARCH GAP?

For businesses. The 2020s have brought a sharp increase in geopolitical risk
exposure for multinational firms. Beyond Russia, rising tensions in the
Taiwan Strait, the fragmentation of US-China economic relations, and
political instability in multiple regions mean that geopolitically forced
exit is no longer a tail risk but an operational planning consideration.
Our research provides a diagnostic framework: firms can assess their
"knowledge exposure" in geopolitically volatile markets by examining the
balance of codified IP versus tacit operational knowledge in their
subsidiaries. IP-heavy operations face a sell-or-lose dilemma under
geopolitical duress -- they can recover value through sale, but doing so
transfers strategic capabilities to adversarial actors. Operationally
embedded firms, paradoxically, face lower knowledge-transfer risk because
their core knowledge walks out the door with their people. Understanding
this asymmetry ex ante allows firms to pre-position exit strategies, adjust
subsidiary governance, and manage knowledge concentration as an element of
geopolitical risk management. Our finding that home-country effects are
among the strongest predictors of exit behavior also alerts firms that their
domicile's political relationships and institutional norms will shape their
degrees of freedom during a geopolitical crisis.

For policymakers. Sanctions are the primary non-military instrument through
which democratic coalitions respond to authoritarian aggression. The nine EU
sanctions packages adopted against Russia in 2022 targeted specific sectors,
but our evidence suggests that sector-level sanctions interact with firm-
level knowledge characteristics in ways that policymakers may not
anticipate. When sanctions compress exit timelines, IP-heavy firms in
sanctioned sectors face intensified pressure to sell quickly -- potentially
at discounted valuations and to buyers aligned with the sanctioned regime.
This creates a perverse channel: the very policy designed to isolate an
autocratic economy may inadvertently accelerate the transfer of Western
proprietary knowledge to that economy. Policymakers designing future
sanctions regimes should consider incorporating knowledge-transfer screening
mechanisms, particularly for divestitures in sectors with high patent
intensity, to ensure that exit-through-sale does not undermine the strategic
objectives of the sanctions themselves.


4.  WHAT DATA AND RESEARCH METHOD WILL BE USED?

4.1 Data

We construct a novel firm-level dataset by merging six distinct sources:

(1) Yale Chief Executive Leadership Institute (CELI) Tracker. The backbone
dataset tracks 1,589 Western firms operating in Russia, assigning each a
withdrawal grade: A (clean break -- suspended operations and announced
exit), B (suspension or scaling back), C (reducing activity), D (buying
time), or F (business as usual). We use 11 tracker snapshots from December
2022 through May 2025. The snapshot sequence also dates each firm's exit
(first snapshot at Grade A), which feeds the hazard analysis. From the
free-text action descriptions we classify exit mode -- sold, seized,
suspended, other -- with state seizures (Danone, Carlsberg, Fortum,
Uniper) hand-coded as a separate competing risk rather than a chosen exit
mode.

(2) Bureau van Dijk Orbis. We extract subsidiary-level financial data for
6,405 Russian subsidiaries of Western MNCs, including total assets,
employment, shareholders' equity, NACE sector codes, incorporation dates,
and active/inactive status. We aggregate to the parent level and merge to
the FULL Yale panel -- all grades A through F, not only exiters --
yielding 1,394 matched firms (88% match rate via fuzzy string matching
with manual overrides), of which 495 are non-exiting stayers. Retaining
the stayers is what permits the selection-corrected design below.

(3) Lens.org Patent Database. We collect patent portfolio counts per
parent, queried from the Lens.org API using applicant/owner name matching.
(Current coverage is the 1,048 Grade A/B exiters with lifetime counts; the
collection script now targets the full panel with a pre-invasion 2017-2021
grant window, to be re-run.)

(4) Bloomberg M&A. We extract 790 M&A deals involving Russian targets and
Western sellers, matching 165 to our sample. Deal announce dates provide
sharp event timing for completed sales; due to low value disclosure we use
the deal indicator as a binary measure rather than a continuous
exit-payoff variable.

(5) EU Sanctions Packages. We compile the EU sanctions packages adopted in
2022 with their adoption dates and map them to NACE 2-digit sectors,
constructing both a cross-sectional exposure count and a time-varying
exposure profile (which packages had hit the firm's sector by date t).

(6) Home-country institutions. Sanctions-coalition membership from the
Russian government's own "unfriendly countries" list (Decree No. 430-r,
March 2022 -- pre-determined and binary), rule of law from the World Bank
Worldwide Governance Indicators (RL.EST), and legal-origin families per
La Porta et al.

4.2 Key Variables: Codified and Tacit Knowledge Intensity

The model is parameterized by two quantities, and we measure them
separately rather than as a composite:

   k (codified):  percentile rank of the parent's patent stock
                  (pat_pctile; within-sector variant available)
   h (tacit):     percentile rank of subsidiary employees / total assets
                  (lab_pctile; within-sector variant available)

An earlier version of this project used a symmetric composite of the two
percentile ranks (a "Knowledge Replicability Index"). We retired it after
a decomposition test showed the patent component does all the work in the
exit-mode margin -- a result the model turns from an anomaly into a
prediction: the sale margin prices what transfers, and what transfers is
the codified component. Planned measurement upgrades (parent-level 2017-
2021 patent vintage scaled by parent size; labor intensity with numerator
and denominator from the same 2019-2021 window) are specified in the
repository.

4.3 Econometric Specifications

Each sub-question is estimated by the design its comparative static
implies. Because firms differ in retention value V_i ~ F (log-concave),
the model delivers a binary-choice likelihood directly.

(a) Exit mode with selection correction (Q1). A Heckman-style two-stage on
the full population of matched parents: a selection probit for completing
a clean exit (exclusion restriction: consumer-facing B2C status, on the
logic that boycott pressure shifts whether to exit but not the mechanics
of the asset transfer), then a sell-versus-walk probit among completed
exits including the inverse Mills ratio. Seizures are dropped from this
margin as a third competing risk. Controls: ln(assets), years in Russia,
ln(subsidiary count), industry fixed effects.

(b) Sanctions amplification (Q2). A pooled interaction,

   P(Sold | exit) = Phi(b1*k + b2*SancExp + b3*(k x SancExp) + X'b),

with b3 the object of interest, plus a cause-specific competing-risks Cox
model (sale and walk-away as competing events) with TIME-VARYING sector
exposure built from the staggered adoption dates of the 2022 EU packages.

(c) Institutional moderators (Q3). Cross-level interactions (k x
coalition, k x rule of law, k x common law) with home-country fixed
effects, which absorb institution levels -- only the interactions are
identified, which is exactly what the question asks. A Mundlak correlated-
random-effects probit serves as robustness.

Inference throughout uses heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors and,
for coefficients where home-country clustering matters (~35 clusters), a
wild cluster bootstrap (Rademacher weights, null imposed).

4.4 Identification Strategy

For the sanctions question, identification comes from WHEN a firm's sector
became exposed -- the staggered rollout of the 2022 EU packages entering
the hazard as a time-varying covariate, conditional on sector composition
-- rather than from a cross-sectional sanctioned/non-sanctioned split.
This directly addresses the concern that sanctions target knowledge-
intensive strategic sectors. For the exit-mode question, the selection
stage addresses conditioning on exiters, and the B2C exclusion restriction
separates the exit decision from the transfer mechanics.

For the full paper, we plan three extensions: (i) firm-level sanction
exposure built from EU Official Journal product-code annexes (CN to NACE
correspondences, dual-use list coverage, counterparty SDN exposure),
replacing the sector-level mapping and extending it past 2022; (ii)
enriching the control set with parent-level characteristics, including
asset tangibility (PP&E/assets), the key sellability confounder; and
(iii) a FIML bivariate probit (heckprobit) replacing the two-step
control-function approximation.


5.  WHAT ARE THE ANTICIPATED RESULTS?

Our preliminary analysis, based on a matched population of 1,394 firms
(899 exiters, 495 stayers; 362 completed Grade A exits with patent data in
the mode margin), yields the following results:

Knowledge-exit mode channel (Prediction 1). Selling rises monotonically
across parent patent-stock terciles: 28%, 36%, 49%. In the mode probit,
codified-knowledge intensity (k) is positive and significant (coefficient
0.49, p = 0.045; average marginal effect about 17 percentage points from
bottom to top of the distribution; wild-cluster-bootstrap p = 0.068 across
36 home-country clusters). Tacit intensity (h) is negative but imprecise.
The inverse Mills ratio is statistically indistinguishable from zero, so
selection into exit does not appear to bias the mode margin. The
complementary hazard evidence is sharper still: in the cause-specific
competing-risks model, patent intensity strongly reduces the walk-away
hazard (log hazard ratio -1.16, p < 0.001) with no significant effect on
the sale hazard -- IP-heavy firms do not abandon.

Sanctions amplification (Prediction 2). At the current sector-level
exposure measure, the k x sanctions interaction is a precise zero. We
flag a cautionary result from our own revision process: an earlier
split-sample comparison suggested the knowledge effect was "4.4x stronger"
in sanctioned sectors, but that ratio did not survive the corrected exit-
mode classification and the population-based measurement -- reinforcing
that the pooled interaction, not a subsample R-squared ratio, is the
statistic that can carry the claim. Statistical power at sector level is
limited (32 sanctioned-sector firms in the mode margin); the planned firm-
level exposure measure built from EU product-code annexes is the sharpest
route to a definitive answer, and the model itself signs both possible
outcomes (threshold channel: positive; buyer-pool channel: negative).

Institutional moderators (Prediction 3). Home-country levels matter --
Japanese firms are about 28 percentage points less likely to make a clean
exit, Finnish firms about 20 points more likely -- but the model's
sharper prediction concerns interactions with knowledge type, and under
home-country fixed effects the k x coalition, k x rule-of-law, and k x
common-law interactions are directionally consistent with the threshold
channel yet statistically insignificant under the wild cluster bootstrap.
The near-uniform coalition membership of the exiter sample (only 8
non-coalition completed exits) limits identification; the stakeholder-
pressure channel awaits the sharper ESG-disclosure-mandate measure.

We anticipate that the planned extensions -- particularly firm-level
sanction exposure, the full-panel patent vintage re-pull, and the
tangibility control -- will decide the sanctions question and tighten the
institutional contrasts.


6.  WHAT ARE THE IMPLICATIONS FOR BUSINESSES, POLICY, AND SOCIETY?

For businesses. This study provides a practical diagnostic: the two-
dimensional knowledge-exposure profile (codified patent intensity versus
tacit labor intensity) offers firms a tool for assessing geopolitical
knowledge exposure across their international subsidiary portfolios. Firms with
concentrated IP in geopolitically volatile markets should pre-plan exit
pathways that minimize involuntary knowledge transfer -- for example, by
structuring subsidiary IP governance so that critical patents and
proprietary processes can be retracted to the parent before or during exit,
rather than being sold with the local entity. The strong home-country
effects further suggest that firms should monitor the geopolitical alignment
between their home government and host countries as a leading indicator of
exit pressure and available exit modes. As geopolitical fragmentation
accelerates, "geopolitical knowledge audits" should become a standard
element of enterprise risk management alongside financial and operational
risk assessments.

For policy. Our findings reveal a tension at the heart of democratic
sanctions design. Sanctions compress exit timelines and raise the cost of
staying, but by doing so, they may push IP-heavy firms toward fire sales
that transfer proprietary knowledge to buyers within the sanctioned regime.
Future sanctions packages could incorporate knowledge-transfer screening --
modeled on existing foreign investment screening mechanisms but applied in
reverse -- to ensure that forced divestitures in strategically sensitive
sectors do not undermine the sanctions' own objectives. More broadly, our
findings inform the design of "smart sanctions" that account for firm-level
heterogeneity rather than treating all firms within a sector identically.

For society and democratic resilience. Democratic societies rely on
sanctions as a primary non-military response to authoritarian aggression.
Our research illuminates a previously unexamined micro-channel through which
the effectiveness of this democratic tool may be undermined: firm-level
knowledge sorting that inadvertently transfers strategic capabilities to
adversarial regimes. By understanding this channel, democratic institutions
can design more effective responses to geopolitical threats, strengthening
the credibility and impact of collective democratic action. In an era when
the robustness of democratic institutions is itself under pressure, ensuring
that the tools of democratic resilience function as intended is not merely
an academic exercise but a matter of urgent practical importance.
