TITLE:
Knowledge Structure and Exit Completion: Why Multinationals Remain
Unresolved in Geopolitically Contested Markets

1.  WHAT IS THE RESEARCH QUESTION?

When a geopolitical crisis makes continued operation in a host country
untenable, announcing an exit is only the beginning of the process. A
multinational may spend months or years waiting for a suitable buyer and the
approvals needed to complete a transaction. During that period it may continue
to bear payroll, legal, and administrative costs while the value of the local
business changes. The company may ultimately sell, close the operation, remain
unresolved, or lose control through state intervention. The paper asks:

   Why do some multinationals complete their departure from a geopolitically
   contested market while others remain unresolved, and does the type of
   knowledge on which the subsidiary depends help explain the duration and
   eventual outcome of the exit process?

We study this question using repeated snapshots of the Yale CELI tracker from
December 2022 through May 2025. The snapshots allow us to distinguish firms
that continued operating, firms that announced an exit but remained
unresolved, firms reported to have completed a sale, firms reported to have
closed, and firms that lost control through state intervention.

The model treats a completed sale as an opportunity that arrives rather than
an action that the parent can take at any moment. While it waits, the parent
pays the cost of maintaining the unresolved subsidiary. Codified assets such
as patents may preserve the value of a future transaction and therefore make
continued waiting worthwhile. Dependence on employees and ongoing operations
may instead raise the cost of waiting. Sanctions and Russian approval rules
may reduce the availability of qualified buyers, lower transaction value, or
delay completion.

Because these conditions change over time, the parent repeatedly compares the
value of continued waiting with the cost of closing. The model therefore
generates predictions about the duration of the unresolved state and the
eventual outcome, rather than assuming a one-time choice between sale and
closure. The mathematical skeleton is provided in `docs/formal_model.md`.


2.  WHAT IS THE RESEARCH GAP AND CONTRIBUTION TO THE ACADEMIC LITERATURE?

This study addresses three gaps at the intersection of international
business, political economy, and the knowledge-based view of the firm.

Gap 1: Geopolitical triggers in the market exit literature. The
international business literature on foreign market exit has focused
predominantly on economic determinants -- declining profitability, rising
liability of foreignness, sunk cost considerations, and host-country
institutional deterioration (Benito and Welch, 1997; Berry, 2013; Soule,
Swaminathan, and Tihanyi, 2014). While some work examines political risk as
a motive for exit (Dai, Eden, and Beamish, 2013; Witte et al., 2020), this
literature has largely treated exit as a binary decision (stay vs. leave)
rather than examining the unresolved period between announcement and
completion. That period is economically important because companies continue
to bear costs and may retain legal ownership, employment, and productive
capacity in the host country. The scale of the Russia withdrawal provides an
unusually large setting in which to study this process. It should not be
treated as a natural experiment because firms, sectors, policy exposure, and
exit timing are not randomly assigned.

Gap 2: Micro-mechanisms of sanctions effectiveness. The political economy
literature on economic sanctions overwhelmingly studies aggregate outcomes:
trade flows, GDP effects, regime behavior change, and welfare costs (Hufbauer
et al., 2007; Felbermayr et al., 2020; Bapat et al., 2013). A growing
literature examines firm-level effects of sanctions on trade and investment
(Crozet and Hinz, 2020; Ahn and Ludema, 2020), but no existing work
examines how sanctions interact with firm-level knowledge characteristics to
shape exit behavior. This gap matters because sanctions are a primary tool
of democratic resilience -- the mechanism through which democratic coalitions
respond to autocratic aggression -- yet their firm-level behavioral channels
remain poorly understood.

Gap 3: The knowledge-based view meets geopolitics. The KBV literature
(Grant, 1996; Kogut and Zander, 1993; Nonaka, 1994) has extensively studied
how knowledge characteristics shape firm boundaries, entry mode, and
subsidiary governance, but has not been applied to the exit context. This is
a significant omission: if knowledge characteristics determine entry mode
(Hennart, 1988; Brouthers, 2002), they should logically also shape exit
mode, particularly when exit is involuntary and time-pressured.

Our intended contribution is to connect these literatures through the value
of waiting. We model codified assets and human-capital dependence separately
because they affect different parts of the exit process. Codified assets may
preserve the value of a future transaction; employee-intensive operations may
raise the cost of remaining unresolved. Parent patent-stock rank and
subsidiary labor intensity are preliminary proxies for those concepts. The
current evidence suggests that higher patent rank is associated with a lower
rate of completing an exit without a sale, not with a detectably higher rate
of completed sales. This pattern motivates the revised model but does not
establish its mechanism.


3.  WHY IS IT IMPORTANT FOR BUSINESSES AND POLICY MAKERS TO CLOSE THIS
    RESEARCH GAP?

For businesses. The 2020s have brought a sharp increase in geopolitical risk
exposure for multinational firms. Beyond Russia, rising tensions in the
Taiwan Strait, the fragmentation of US-China economic relations, and
political instability in multiple regions mean that geopolitically forced
exit is no longer a tail risk but an operational planning consideration.
The framework encourages firms to ask which subsidiary assets a buyer could
actually retain after a forced transfer. Patents, licenses, physical assets,
employee know-how, supplier relationships, and parent-provided services may
have very different degrees of transferability. Mapping those dependencies
before a crisis can improve contingency planning and subsidiary governance.
The current estimates, however, are not yet a validated risk-scoring tool and
should not be presented as one. Home-country differences in exit behavior are
large in the sample, but those differences may reflect unobserved firm and
country characteristics rather than institutional mechanisms alone.

For policymakers. Sanctions are the primary non-military instrument through
which democratic coalitions respond to authoritarian aggression. The nine EU
sanctions packages adopted against Russia in 2022 targeted specific sectors.
Sanctions can restrict the buyer pool, require licenses, impose mandatory
discounts, and shorten the time available to negotiate. Those changes may
reduce sales overall, may make transferable assets especially important to
the remaining buyers, or may destroy the value of technology assets when no
capable buyer remains. The current sector-level interaction estimate is close
to zero and therefore does not show that sanctions accelerate proprietary
knowledge transfer. Knowledge-transfer screening may still be worth studying,
but the policy recommendation should remain conditional until firm-level
sanctions exposure and subsidiary-level asset ownership are measured.


4.  WHAT DATA AND RESEARCH METHOD WILL BE USED?

4.1 Data

We construct a novel firm-level dataset by merging six distinct sources:

(1) Yale Chief Executive Leadership Institute (CELI) Tracker. The backbone
dataset tracks 1,589 Western firms operating in Russia, assigning each a
withdrawal grade: A (clean break -- suspended operations and announced
exit), B (suspension or scaling back), C (reducing activity), D (buying
time), or F (business as usual). We use 11 tracker snapshots from December
2022 through May 2025. The snapshot sequence also dates each firm's exit
(first snapshot at Grade A), which feeds the hazard analysis. From the
free-text action descriptions we provisionally classify each firm as still
operating, exit announced but unresolved, sale completed, closure completed,
or subject to state-imposed loss of control. Danone, Carlsberg, Fortum, and
Uniper are manually coded in the last category because state intervention is
not a voluntary corporate outcome.

(2) Bureau van Dijk Orbis. We extract subsidiary-level financial data for
6,405 Russian subsidiaries of Western MNCs, including total assets,
employment, shareholders' equity, NACE sector codes, incorporation dates,
and active/inactive status. We aggregate to the parent level and merge to
the FULL Yale panel -- all grades A through F, not only exiters --
yielding 1,394 matched firms (88% match rate via fuzzy string matching
with manual overrides), of which 495 are non-exiting stayers. Retaining
the stayers is what permits the selection-corrected design below.

(3) Lens.org Patent Database. We collect patent portfolio counts per
parent, queried from the Lens.org API using applicant/owner name matching.
(Current coverage is the 1,048 Grade A/B exiters with lifetime counts; the
collection script now targets the full panel with a pre-invasion 2017-2021
grant window, to be re-run.)

(4) Bloomberg M&A. We extract 790 M&A deals involving Russian targets and
Western sellers, matching 165 to our sample. Deal announce dates provide
sharp event timing for completed sales; due to low value disclosure we use
the deal indicator as a binary measure rather than a continuous
exit-payoff variable.

(5) EU Sanctions Packages. We compile the EU sanctions packages adopted in
2022 with their adoption dates and map them to NACE 2-digit sectors,
constructing both a cross-sectional exposure count and a time-varying
exposure profile (which packages had hit the firm's sector by date t).

(6) Home-country institutions. Sanctions-coalition membership from the
Russian government's own "unfriendly countries" list (Decree No. 430-r,
March 2022 -- pre-determined and binary), rule of law from the World Bank
Worldwide Governance Indicators (RL.EST), and legal-origin families per
La Porta et al.

4.2 Key Variables: Codified and Tacit Knowledge Intensity

The model contains two distinct concepts, which we proxy separately:

   k (transferable codified assets): percentile rank of the parent's patent stock
                  (pat_pctile; within-sector variant available)
   h (human-capital dependence): percentile rank of subsidiary employees / total assets
                  (lab_pctile; within-sector variant available)

These measures do not correspond exactly to the model. Parent patent stock
does not identify intellectual property owned by, licensed to, or transferred
with the Russian subsidiary. Employees divided by assets measures labor
intensity, not the tacitness, firm specificity, or post-sale retention of
employee knowledge. An earlier composite index obscured these differences and
has therefore been retired. Planned improvements include a pre-invasion patent
window scaled by parent size, subsidiary-level IP ownership or licensing where
available, and consistently dated labor and asset measures.

4.3 Empirical Design

The repeated Yale snapshots are organized as a sequence of firm states. A
firm can continue operating, announce an exit but remain unresolved, complete
a sale, complete a closure, or lose control through state intervention. The
primary sample begins when a firm first enters the unresolved state and ends
when a completed outcome is observed or the final snapshot is reached.

The main analysis estimates four quantities that have direct economic
interpretations: expected time from announcement to completion; probability
of remaining unresolved after 6, 12, and 24 months; probability that the next
observed outcome is a sale; and probability that it is a closure. The
statistical implementation uses separate transition equations for the
possible outcomes, but results will be reported as waiting times and predicted
probabilities rather than estimator terminology.

Parent patent rank tests whether codified assets are associated with a lower
probability of closure while the firm waits. Subsidiary employees/assets tests
whether labor-intensive operations are associated with faster closure.
Sector sanctions exposure is retained as a provisional measure of changes in
buyer availability or transaction value. State intervention is modeled as a
separate outcome because it is not a voluntary corporate choice.

The earlier two-stage sale-versus-non-sale model and the cross-sectional
sanctions interactions will be retained as comparisons, not as the paper's
main specifications.

4.4 Identification Strategy

The design is observational. Identification comes from changes in reported
firm states across snapshots and differences in pre-invasion firm
characteristics. It does not establish causality if unmeasured subsidiary
quality affects both knowledge structure and completion. Timing is also
coarse: many firms were already classified as completed exits in the first
available snapshot. The next build therefore prioritizes a full-panel
pre-invasion patent measure and adds PP&E/assets, intangible assets,
profitability, and parent size. Sanctions and institutional interactions will
remain secondary unless their measurement improves.


5.  WHAT ARE THE ANTICIPATED RESULTS?

Our preliminary analysis uses a matched population of 1,394 firms. The
current duration sample contains 709 firms with patent data and is effectively
limited to companies that announced an exit; this selected sample must be
expanded before the revised design is complete.

Knowledge and completion (Prediction 1). Moving from the bottom to the top of
the parent patent ranking is associated with an approximately 69% lower rate
of completing an exit without a sale (p < 0.001). Its estimated relationship
with the rate of completed sales is small relative to its uncertainty
(p = 0.54). Moving across the labor-intensity ranking is associated with an
estimated 32% higher rate of non-sale completion, but this estimate is too
imprecise to support a firm conclusion (p = 0.30). These patterns are
consistent with the idea that codified assets raise the value of waiting and
that employee-intensive operations are more costly to maintain. They do not
establish either mechanism.

Sanctions and completion conditions (Prediction 2). At the current sector-level
exposure measure, the estimated k x sanctions interaction is close to zero. We
flag a cautionary result from our own revision process: an earlier
split-sample comparison suggested the knowledge effect was "4.4x stronger"
in sanctioned sectors, but that ratio did not survive the corrected exit-
mode classification and the population-based measurement -- reinforcing
that the pooled interaction, not a subsample R-squared ratio, is the
statistic that can carry the claim. Statistical power at sector level is
limited (32 sanctioned-sector firms in the mode margin); the planned firm-
level exposure measure built from EU product-code annexes is the sharpest
route to a definitive answer, and the model itself signs both possible
outcomes (threshold channel: positive; buyer-pool channel: negative).

Institutional moderators (Prediction 3). Home-country levels matter --
Japanese firms are about 28 percentage points less likely to make a clean
exit, Finnish firms about 20 points more likely -- but the model's
sharper prediction concerns interactions with knowledge type, and under
home-country fixed effects the k x coalition, k x rule-of-law, and k x
common-law interactions are directionally consistent with the threshold
channel yet statistically insignificant under the wild cluster bootstrap.
The near-uniform coalition membership of the exiter sample (only 8
non-coalition completed exits) limits identification; the stakeholder-
pressure channel awaits the sharper ESG-disclosure-mandate measure.

The main conclusion at this stage is deliberately limited: parent patent rank
predicts the timing of non-sale completion in the current selected sample,
whereas the labor, sanctions, and institutional estimates are imprecise or
close to zero. A full-panel pre-invasion patent measure, clearer state coding,
subsidiary-level IP ownership, and asset-tangibility controls are necessary
before interpreting the association as causal.


6.  WHAT ARE THE IMPLICATIONS FOR BUSINESSES, POLICY, AND SOCIETY?

For businesses. This study provides a practical diagnostic: the two-
dimensional knowledge-exposure profile (codified patent intensity versus
tacit labor intensity) offers firms a tool for assessing geopolitical
knowledge exposure across their international subsidiary portfolios. Firms with
concentrated IP in geopolitically volatile markets should pre-plan exit
pathways that minimize involuntary knowledge transfer -- for example, by
structuring subsidiary IP governance so that critical patents and
proprietary processes can be retracted to the parent before or during exit,
rather than being sold with the local entity. The strong home-country
effects further suggest that firms should monitor the geopolitical alignment
between their home government and host countries as a leading indicator of
exit pressure and available completion paths. As geopolitical fragmentation
accelerates, "geopolitical knowledge audits" should become a standard
element of enterprise risk management alongside financial and operational
risk assessments.

For policy. The model identifies a possible tension in sanctions design.
Restrictions that shrink the buyer pool may lower sale values, while rapid
divestiture requirements may increase pressure to accept the remaining bids.
Whether this combination transfers more strategic knowledge to domestic
buyers is an open empirical question: the current sanctions interaction is
approximately zero. The appropriate recommendation is therefore to collect
transaction-level evidence on the assets and licenses transferred and to
evaluate knowledge-transfer screening, rather than to claim that the present
analysis has already demonstrated a harmful sanctions channel.

For society and democratic resilience. Democratic societies rely on
sanctions as a primary non-military response to authoritarian aggression.
This research evaluates a possible firm-level channel through which sanctions
could have unintended consequences: the assets that remain transferable when
foreign owners leave. Establishing whether that channel is economically
important requires substantially better measures than the current sector and
parent-level proxies. A credible answer would help democratic institutions
design divestiture rules that preserve sanctions objectives without assuming
that every sale transfers the same type or amount of productive knowledge.
