PROPOSAL

TITLE

Corporate Exit Under Geopolitical Distortions: Buyer Scarcity, State Approval,
and the Fate of Foreign Productive Capacity


SUMMARY

The public debate over corporate withdrawal from Russia has counted
announcements: which companies promised to leave, which stayed, and which
appeared to reverse course. I argue that this emphasis misses the economic
problem. Once a company decides to leave, it cannot simply choose a completed
sale. It must find an eligible buyer, accept a price shaped by mandatory
discounts and taxes, and obtain government approval. Until then, the company
remains responsible for a subsidiary whose employees, assets, and legal
obligations do not disappear with the announcement.

This study treats geopolitical exit as a distorted market for corporate
control. A foreign parent waits for a qualified buyer while paying the cost of
maintaining the subsidiary. Patents, licenses, and documented processes may
preserve the value of a future transaction. Employee-intensive operations may
be more costly to maintain during a prolonged suspension. Sanctions can reduce
the pool of eligible buyers, while host-government rules can delay approval or
lower the price the seller is allowed to receive. The same policy may therefore
encourage departure while making departure harder to complete.

What drew me to this problem was the gap visible in repeated company trackers:
public withdrawal announcements accumulated quickly, while many ownership and
operating arrangements remained unresolved years later.

The ideal study would combine dated company announcements with Russian legal
ownership records, transaction prices and buyers, government approval dates,
subsidiary financial accounts, employment and payroll, patents and licenses,
and firm-specific sanctions exposure. These data would support a dynamic model
of waiting, sale, closure, and state intervention. The model would separate
three forces: the scarcity of buyers, the value preserved in a transfer, and
the cost of remaining unresolved. Counterfactual exercises would ask how much
faster exits would have occurred without approval delays, mandatory discounts,
or sanctions-related buyer restrictions, and who would have borne the resulting
losses.

My position is that sanctions and corporate-exit rules should be evaluated not
by the number of withdrawal announcements but by what happens to productive
capacity afterward. A sale, a closure, and a subsidiary held in limbo have
different consequences for workers, shareholders, tax revenues, and the host
state. Making those differences visible would give managers more realistic
contingency plans and help policymakers design divestiture rules that do not
undermine their own strategic objectives.


1. THE PROBLEM

Geopolitical crises are usually described as shocks that force companies to
choose whether to remain in a market. That description is incomplete. Leaving
is a process, not a switch. The parent may announce a withdrawal quickly, but
completion requires a buyer, a viable transaction, and permission to transfer
control. During the interval between announcement and completion, the firm may
continue to employ workers, hold assets, pay taxes, fund basic operations, and
bear legal responsibility for the subsidiary.

Russia after the February 2022 invasion of Ukraine makes this problem visible.
Foreign parents faced sanctions from their home jurisdictions and restrictions
from the Russian state. Those two sets of policies did not simply increase the
desire to exit. They altered the market through which exit had to occur.
Potential buyers became scarcer. Transaction prices were subject to discounts,
taxes, and bargaining pressure. Approval requirements added delay and
uncertainty. In some cases, the state assumed control before the parent could
complete a voluntary transaction.

The central research question is:

    How do geopolitical restrictions reshape the market for corporate control,
    and how do those distortions determine the time required to complete an
    exit and the eventual allocation of productive assets?

This formulation moves beyond a count of corporate promises. It asks what
happened to factories, stores, employees, intellectual property, and tax-paying
entities after the promise was made.


2. THE ECONOMIC MECHANISM

The project begins with one friction: a parent that wants to leave cannot sell
until a qualified buyer and the necessary approval become available. While it
waits, it pays the cost of maintaining the unresolved subsidiary. It can stop
waiting by closing the operation, but closure is costly and irreversible.

Three quantities organize the analysis.

First, buyer availability determines how quickly a feasible transaction can
arrive. Sanctions, financing restrictions, and approval rules can reduce this
arrival rate.

Second, transferable value determines what an eligible buyer is willing to
pay. Physical assets, patents, licenses, documented processes, customer
relationships, and continued access to the parent need not survive a change in
control to the same degree. Mandatory discounts and transaction taxes reduce
the amount the parent can recover.

Third, carrying cost determines how expensive it is to remain unresolved.
Payroll, leases, compliance obligations, maintenance, and the deterioration of
idle assets may make waiting increasingly costly. Employee-intensive operations
may be particularly expensive to preserve during suspension.

At each date, the parent compares the value of continued waiting with the cost
of closing. Conditions change over time, so the decision is genuinely dynamic.
A policy that reduces buyer availability can delay sales and increase the
number of unresolved subsidiaries. A policy that reduces the permitted price
can make closure more attractive. A rise in carrying costs can bring forward
closure even when a future buyer remains possible.

The model generates four empirical implications:

1. Assets that preserve value under new ownership should reduce the rate of
   closure while the parent waits.
2. High carrying costs should shorten the unresolved period and increase the
   probability of closure.
3. Restrictions on buyers and approvals should delay completed sales.
4. A characteristic can increase the eventual probability of sale by reducing
   closure, even if it does not increase the rate at which buyers arrive.

The formal skeleton is developed in `docs/formal_model.md`.


3. IDEAL DATA

The proposal is written around the data required to answer the question well,
not around the limitations of the current repository. The ideal unit of
observation is a foreign-parent-by-Russian-subsidiary-by-month panel beginning
before February 2022 and continuing until legal separation, liquidation, state
intervention, or the end of observation.

The ideal dataset would combine the following sources.

Corporate intentions and operating status. Dated company statements and
archived trackers would identify the first announcement of suspension,
reduction, or intended departure. These records measure intent, not completion.

Legal ownership and entity status. Historical Russian corporate-register data,
commercial registry services, and successive Orbis vintages would identify
changes in direct and ultimate ownership, liquidation dates, name changes, and
temporary administration. These records provide the legal completion outcome.

Transactions and buyers. Bloomberg, Refinitiv, Mergermarket, company filings,
and buyer disclosures would provide announcement and completion dates,
transaction prices, ownership shares, payment terms, repurchase options, and
buyer identity. Buyer balance sheets and industry experience would measure
whether the buyer was capable of maintaining the operation.

Government approval. Application dates, approval dates, denials, conditions,
valuation requirements, mandatory discounts, and exit levies would measure the
permission constraint. Where individual decisions are not public, the study
would construct verified transaction histories and use policy-regime dates,
while reporting missing approval information explicitly.

Subsidiary operations. Pre-invasion and post-invasion accounts would provide
revenue, profitability, payroll, employment, PP&E, intangible assets, cash,
debt, inventories, taxes, and parent-company support. These variables measure
carrying costs, deterioration, and the productive capacity at stake.

Knowledge and organizational dependence. Patent ownership and licensing,
trademarks, software rights, R&D personnel, parent-subsidiary service
agreements, and the share of inputs supplied by the parent would distinguish
assets that can transfer from capabilities that depend on continued
relationships. Parent patent counts alone would be treated as a fallback proxy,
not as the preferred measure.

Policy exposure. Transaction-level sanctions, financing restrictions,
counterparty designations, export controls, and sector rules would measure the
constraints faced by each seller and potential buyer. Broad sector indicators
would be used only when more precise exposure is unavailable.

Workers and the host economy. Employment, payroll, production, taxes, and
plant-level operation after transfer or closure would show who bears the
consequences of each exit path.


4. EMPIRICAL AND QUANTITATIVE DESIGN

The empirical work would proceed in four stages.

First, the study would document the exit process. It would report the time from
announcement to completed sale, closure, or state intervention; the share of
firms still unresolved after 6, 12, and 24 months; and the productive capacity
associated with each state.

Second, it would estimate how buyer availability, transferable value, carrying
costs, and approval status predict transitions between states. The analysis
would be reported through expected waiting times and predicted probabilities,
with technical estimator details placed in the methods appendix.

Third, the model would be estimated using moments that directly correspond to
its mechanisms: the distribution of waiting times; transaction prices relative
to pre-invasion value; closure rates; approval delays; employment and asset
retention after transfer; and differences across firms with varying asset and
knowledge structures.

Fourth, the estimated model would conduct counterfactual experiments. These
would remove one distortion at a time: approval delay, mandatory price discount,
exit levy, sanctions-related buyer restrictions, or elevated carrying costs.
The analysis would decompose each policy's effects on completion time,
transaction value, closure, employment, tax payments, and the amount of
productive capacity retained in Russia.

The purpose of the model is not to replace transparent evidence. It is to
separate mechanisms that produce similar observed delays. A firm may remain
unresolved because no buyer is available, because approval is pending, because
the permitted price is too low, or because the parent still finds waiting less
costly than closure. Observed status alone cannot distinguish those explanations.


5. CONTRIBUTION AND POINT OF VIEW

Research on foreign divestment commonly treats exit as a decision made by the
parent. Research on sanctions commonly evaluates aggregate trade, investment,
or political outcomes. This project connects the two by treating divestiture as
a market whose operation is shaped by both home- and host-country policy.

The broader point is normative as well as analytical. Governments and public
trackers often treat an announced withdrawal as evidence of alignment with
sanctions objectives. That is too easy. An announcement can be followed by a
discounted transfer of productive capacity to a domestic buyer, a costly
closure borne by workers, years of continued tax payments during unresolved
ownership, or an involuntary seizure. These outcomes are not interchangeable.

My position is that corporate-exit policy should be judged by the post-exit
allocation it creates, not by announcement counts. Policymakers should ask
whether a rule deprives the host state of strategic capacity, transfers that
capacity to a new owner, leaves it operating under unresolved foreign
ownership, or destroys value that primarily belonged to workers and creditors.

This perspective also changes managerial advice. A credible geopolitical exit
plan must prepare for an extended period in which the company wants to leave
but cannot complete a transaction. Firms should map which assets can be
separated, which licenses and services a buyer would need, how long payroll and
maintenance can be sustained, and what governance authority local managers
retain if communication with the parent is disrupted.


6. INVITATION TO CONVERSATION

The project is intended to bring managers, workers, policymakers, and scholars
into the same conversation. Managers understand that exit is operationally
messy. Workers experience the consequences of suspension, sale, and closure.
Sanctions officials care about strategic isolation. Host governments can use
approval and administration rules to influence who receives the assets and at
what price.

The provocative question is not whether companies should leave an aggressor
state. It is whether the rules intended to compel or control departure produce
the outcomes their designers actually want. A policy can be morally clear and
operationally self-defeating. The goal of this study is to make that possibility
measurable and therefore open to better design.


7. CURRENT DRAFT STATUS

The present repository provides an initial descriptive foundation rather than
the ideal study. Repeated Yale snapshots supply dated statements and changing
grades. Orbis provides subsidiary assets and employees. Bloomberg supplies
some transaction records, and Lens.org supplies incomplete parent patent
counts. These data reveal a substantial unresolved state and suggest that
parent patent rank is associated with a lower rate of non-sale completion.

They do not yet observe government applications, reliable legal completion for
every subsidiary, monthly carrying costs, subsidiary-level intellectual
property, or the full set of firms at risk. The existing estimates should
therefore be presented as motivation for the ideal design, not as its final
results. The README documents these limitations and the current reproducible
pipeline in detail.


SELECTED INTELLECTUAL FOUNDATIONS

The final paper would build on research on foreign divestment, economic
statecraft and sanctions, search and matching, fire sales, firm boundaries,
and the knowledge-based view of the firm. Its quantitative architecture is
inspired by research that uses a disciplined model to connect an institutional
distortion to observed microeconomic behavior and then evaluates policy through
counterfactual comparisons. The relevant references will be expanded only
after the paper's focal outlet and contribution are fixed.
